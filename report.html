MissionLedger

An AI-assisted budgeting app for households, nonprofits, and small businesses — expenses get reviewed automatically, rule violations get caught before they become a problem, and tax-time paperwork gets organized in one click.

Author: Namya Sharma (MGT 409, AI Foundations for Managers)
GitHub repo: github.com/Namya0909/missionledger
Live app: missionledger.onrender.com — sleeps after ~15 min idle on Render's free tier; first load after a quiet spell takes 30-60s to wake up.

1. Motivation

MissionLedger exists for three different people who, on the surface, have nothing in common — a group of roommates splitting rent, a nonprofit treasurer tracking restricted grants, and a small-business owner watching sales and payroll — but who share the exact same underlying problem: tracking shared or regulated money is tedious, error-prone, and nobody enjoys doing it, so it gets done badly or not at all.

Couples, roommates, and groups

Money is one of the most common sources of conflict between people who share a household. In Fidelity Investments' 2024 Couples & Money Study, more than 45% of couples admitted they argue about finances at least occasionally, and 1 in 4 named money as the single greatest challenge in their relationship. A separate Ipsos survey found that 1 in 3 (34%) partnered Americans identify money as a source of conflict in their relationship, and Empower's research names spending habits (38%) and budgeting (33%) as the specific topics most likely to trigger a disagreement.

The tools that exist today (Splitwise and similar apps) solve recording who paid for what — they don't solve enforcing a shared budget or flagging when something looks wrong. Two people can agree on a grocery budget and still fight about it three months later because nobody noticed it was blown in month one. That's a software gap, not just a discipline gap.

Nonprofits

This part isn't abstract for me. Before this program, I volunteered with several NGOs in India — Make A Difference (MAD), the Lighthouse Project, and Abhigyaan — and the one thing all three had in common was a chronic shortage of funds. At MAD, I taught Sociology and English to 11th-grade girls at one of their shelter homes in Mumbai; beyond teaching, our volunteer team also helped cover their rent, funded their continued education, and mentored them once they aged out of the shelter at 18. Supporting all the children across MAD's Mumbai shelter homes takes upward of ₹6 lakh a year, and raising that is genuinely hard — I was one of MAD's top three fundraisers for 2023. But the part that stuck with me more was what happened after the money came in: even with real funds on hand, we'd regularly struggle to manage and optimize them — knowing which rupee was earmarked for whose rent versus whose tuition versus whose mentorship support, and whether we were actually staying within any of those lines, was a constant, spreadsheet-and-goodwill struggle, not something a system was helping with. That gap — raising money is hard, but tracking and allocating it responsibly once it's in hand is a separate, largely unsolved problem — is the same gap the statistics below point to at scale, and it's the real reason the nonprofit side of this app exists, not just a hypothetical use case.

Zooming out to the broader numbers: small nonprofits are expected to report their spending by IRS Form 990 category (Program Services, Management & General, Fundraising) — but most of them structurally can't produce that breakdown on demand. A joint Urban Institute / Indiana University Nonprofit Overhead Cost Study of roughly 1,500 nonprofits found that barely one-third track staff time by functional expense category per payroll period at all; among small organizations specifically, only 22% do. An older (2008) Queensland University of Technology study — dated, and specific to one country's government grants, but directionally useful — put the average paperwork burden for a small nonprofit at nearly 144 hours a year, with the load falling hardest on the smallest organizations with the least staff to spare.

Small businesses (MSMEs)

LegalZoom's 2026 small-business survey found that nearly half of owners say compliance is taking more time than it did the year before, and 32% spend up to 20 hours a month on compliance-related tasks alone. A separate 2023 survey of US entrepreneurs found administrative work eating roughly 36% of a 45.5-hour work week — about 16 hours. On the tax side specifically, IRS-published burden estimates put a small pass-through business return at roughly 50 hours and $4,200 in out-of-pocket cost per year, and a small employer issuing even 1-5 W-2s at another ~16 hours and $620. None of that is the actual tax strategy work an accountant adds value on — it's gathering, organizing, and double-checking numbers that should already be sitting in one place.

2. What Is New

Budgeting software is a crowded space, and I want to be honest about what already exists: Splitwise (and its many clones) for splitting group expenses, Mint/YNAB for personal budgeting, QuickBooks/Wave/FreshBooks for small-business bookkeeping, and grant-management SaaS like Foundant or Bloomerang for nonprofits. ChatGPT itself can already explain what a 990 form is or draft a donor thank-you letter if you paste in the numbers by hand.

What none of those do, together, is:

  • Serve all three very different organization types under one shared rules/permissions engine instead of three separate products — a group's "split by custom share," a nonprofit's "this category isn't allowed for this grant," and a business's "payroll always needs approval" are all the same underlying per-pot approval/rule system, just configured differently.
  • Catch a rule violation proactively, not just record a transaction. The Rules Checker (Section 4) runs Python logic — not an AI guess — against every new expense and flags over-budget spending, a grant-disallowed category, a goal falling behind pace, or payroll not covered, the moment it happens, with an AI-written plain-English explanation of why.
  • Organize (not file) tax-time paperwork specific to the workspace type — the Tax Prep Pack (Section 4) is, as far as I could find, not something any of the consumer apps above do at all: a Form 990 filing-tier recommendation with a reason, draft donor acknowledgment letters with the IRS-required legal language inserted by Python (not the AI), and a small-business sales-tax worksheet with a 1099-NEC candidate list.
  • Show its own cost and security posture to the user, not just the developer — a Costs page anyone with the right role can open, and a Security Log that records every time a defense actually fires.

I'm not claiming the core idea — "an AI reviews your expenses and answers questions about your money" — is itself unprecedented; several fintech products do something adjacent. The specific combination above (one unified rules engine across three org types, a Python-only rule-violation detector, and a tax-document organizer with compliance-grade fixed legal text) is what I believe is genuinely new here.

3. The Agents

Every agent in MissionLedger follows the same rule: Python computes every number and enforces every rule; AI only reads, sorts, and explains. No AI call ever writes to the database except logging one new expense, and that expense always starts "pending" for a human to approve — the AI itself can never approve anything.

Agent roster

  • Gatekeeper — screens every chat message before anything else runs; blocks prompt injection and off-topic requests.
  • Orchestrator — decides what a chat message needs: a question goes to the Analyst, "log this expense" goes through the one write-capable tool, a report request gets pointed at the Reports tab. Capped at 6 tool round-trips so it can't loop.
  • Analyst — answers questions like "how much is left in Groceries?" using read-only tools (list_pots, list_transactions, compute_settle_up, …); every number it states is checked against the tool output it just received, within $1, or replaced with a safe fallback.
  • Rules Checker — explains, in plain language, a rule violation that rules.py (pure Python) already detected.
  • Intake — reads a bank statement CSV, a receipt photo, or a grant agreement PDF and stages what it found in an editable table; nothing is saved until a human confirms it.
  • Reporter — writes the narrative sentences around numbers reports.py already computed, for the one-button PDF report.
  • Tax Pack — same pattern as the Reporter, for the Tax Prep Pack; the one place it's AI-generated text is wrapped around a fixed Python template for the IRS-required donor-disclosure sentence, never freely generated.
  • Setup Agent — a short conversation (max 4 questions) that turns one sentence ("I run a coffee shop…") into an editable plan; every dollar calculation (savings feasibility, sales-tax pot sizing) is computed by Python, the AI only explains it.

Architecture

Model choice

Every agent uses gpt-6-luna via the course's Portkey-proxied key (PORTKEY_API_KEY), per the course's model rules. ai/core.py is the single choke point every one of the 8 agents above is built through, which is also where the global token budget and per-call token/cost logging live (Section 5).

What the five cost-saving measures actually do

  • Cache-friendly prompts — keep each agent's unchanging instructions as a stable prefix so the provider can cache and discount repeated tokens (the naive baseline rebuilds the prompt fresh every time, the common mistake this fixes).
  • Short Gatekeeper — a condensed screening prompt capped at ~8 words of output.
  • Python prefilter — answers simple "how much is left in <pot>" questions straight from the database, skipping the Gatekeeper and Orchestrator entirely.
  • Category totals — sends the AI category subtotals instead of every individual transaction once there are more than a few.
  • Response cache — reuses the answer to an identical question asked again within 10 minutes instead of calling the AI again.

Did cost-cutting hurt performance?

Yes, measurably, once: with every measure on, the benchmark's accuracy dropped from 20/20 to 19/20 — one question (an all-time category breakdown requiring every transaction, not just a subtotal) got a slightly less precise answer under the "category totals" simplification. That's disclosed here rather than hidden; a 77% cost reduction ($0.0135 → $0.0031 per run, a 4.4× saving) for a 5% hit on one edge-case question type is a trade I judged worth making for a budgeting app, but it is a real trade, not a free lunch.

An important honesty note on the price list itself

Every dollar figure in this report (and in the app's own Costs page) is computed from the real token counts the API returns, multiplied by that estimate — the token counts are solid; the dollar conversion is only as good as the placeholder rate until the real one is confirmed.

5. Safety

Since this handles people's financial data, every AI helper sits behind layered defenses, and every time one of them actually fires, it's written to a Security Log the workspace's admin/treasurer/owner can review.

Risks covered

  • Prompt injection — everything pulled from an uploaded file (statement rows, receipt text, PDF text) is wrapped in explicit untrusted-data tags telling every agent that no text inside it, no matter what it says, can change its role, approve anything, or switch workspaces.
  • Agent malfunction — the Orchestrator is capped at 6 tool round-trips (can't loop forever); every dollar amount the Analyst states is checked against the real tool result from that turn, within $1, or replaced with a safe fallback and logged.
  • Overspending — 200,000 tokens/user/day, and (new this session) a 2,000,000 tokens/day ceiling shared across the whole class's course key — if hit, every AI feature pauses with a friendly message while the rest of the app (pots, transactions, approvals) keeps working exactly as normal.
  • Account takeover — bcrypt password hashing, a 5-wrong-attempts/15-minute throttle, a 10-strikes persistent lockout, and a second factor: at signup each user picks 1 security question from a pool of 10 (answered from a strict dropdown, not free text), and logging in requires the password and that answer. Dropped from an earlier 3-question design to cut signup/login friction, which is honestly a weaker guess-resistance bar on its own (1-in-10 per attempt instead of 1-in-1,000) — the same rate limit/lockout above is what actually makes guessing impractical, not secrecy of the answer list. The same question backs account recovery (forgotten-password reset): it re-verifies the answer with the identical rate-limited budget as a wrong login, and returns a real-looking question whether or not the email has an account, so the response never reveals who has signed up.
  • Document exposure — a transaction's attached receipt (the only file type this app persists to disk) is encrypted at rest (Fernet/AES) before being written, decrypted only for an authorized, authenticated request; every image/PDF upload is also checked against its real file signature, not just its claimed Content-Type, so a renamed file can't slip past the type check.
  • Trivial accounts — signup rejects a short list of the most common leaked passwords outright.

Evidence: the 12-attack suite, 12/12 passing

A standalone test suite (also runnable as real pytest tests, no server to start by hand) exercises the live app against 12 real attacks: prompt injection via a statement/receipt/grant upload, cross-workspace impersonation, investment-advice requests, PII leakage, a forced 50-call tool loop, a login/chat flood, oversized/wrong-type uploads, and the number-verification guard.

6. Impact

Why this should matter (secondary-source evidence, projected — not yet measured on MissionLedger itself)

Translating the Section 1 statistics into a plausible mechanism for each audience:

  • Couples/groups: with 34-45% of partnered people naming money as a conflict source and spending/budgeting as the top-cited triggers (Fidelity; Ipsos; Empower), a shared system that enforces the same budget view for both people and flags a rule violation automatically (rather than one person noticing it weeks later) targets the specific ambiguity those surveys point to — this is a plausible mechanism, not a measured reduction in arguments.
  • Nonprofits: since only 22% of small nonprofits track spending by functional (990) category per pay period at all (Urban Institute/Indiana University), the Tax Prep Pack's automatic Program/Management/Fundraising bucketing and filing-tier recommendation replaces work that most small nonprofits aren't doing today, not work they're doing slowly — for those organizations the realistic gain is going from "can't produce this breakdown" to "can produce it with one click," which is a different, probably larger, kind of improvement than a time-savings percentage.
  • Small businesses: IRS burden estimates put a small pass-through return at ~50 hours and $4,200 of the owner's own cost per year, separate from accountant fees; the Tax Prep Pack's sales-tax worksheet and 1099-NEC candidate list target the document-gathering portion of that burden specifically — a reasonable estimate, not a filing-time measurement, since the app organizes paperwork and explicitly does not file or give tax advice.

Pilot feedback

The app shipped a role-aware Feedback form (Figure 12) specifically to collect this kind of evidence: a tester self-identifies as a Student, Corporate Professional, Accounting/Tax Professional, or Other, then answers a tailored set of questions for that role — a corporate professional judging whether they’d trust and recommend it, a tax/accounting professional judging the Tax Prep Pack's real-world credibility. In the time available for this project, real third-party pilot responses didn't come in, so this report doesn't claim measured feedback it doesn't have. The Impact case above rests on secondary research and, for the nonprofit side specifically, the firsthand volunteer experience described in Section 1 — not on a completed pilot. If real responses arrive after submission, they belong here, replacing this paragraph with their actual ratings and quotes rather than projection.

7. Limits and Next Steps

Honest gaps, in order of how much they'd matter to someone actually relying on this:

  • Caught by real-device testing, already fixed: testing on an actual phone surfaced two bugs before submission — a couple of mobile layout issues (a two-column form squeezing on a narrow screen, tab labels clipping instead of scrolling) and a confusing raw “User not found” error on Settle Up/Recurring Charges, traced to Render's free-tier ephemeral disk invalidating an already-logged-in session mid-use. All three are fixed: the forms are now responsive, and any session invalidated this way now logs out cleanly with a friendly “your session expired” message instead of a raw error on whichever page happened to make the next request.
  • No real pilot data — real third-party pilot responses didn't come in during this project's timeline, so the Impact section above rests on secondary-source projection and, for the nonprofit case, firsthand volunteer experience (Section 1) rather than measurement; see Section 6.
  • Ephemeral storage on the free hosting tier — fix written, not yet confirmed live: Render's free web-service plan has no persistent disk, so every restart/redeploy silently wiped real signups and their security-question answers back to just the demo data — the exact bug a real user hit. The code fix (move the database off that disk entirely, onto Render's separate managed free Postgres instance, which survives web-service redeploys) is written and pushed, but the Render redeploy needed to actually pick it up didn't go through, so the live site may still be running on the old ephemeral SQLite file until that deploy is sorted out on the Render dashboard side. Even once deployed, uploaded receipt files (still written to the web service's own disk) remain unaddressed — a real org would need object storage for those — and Render's free Postgres plan itself expires 30 days after creation (14-day grace period before deletion), fine for a course project's lifetime but worth re-checking if this needs to stay live longer.
  • Invite links were broken, already fixed: the shareable link a new workspace generated to invite others was built from the server's own guess at its public address, which is wrong behind Render's reverse proxy (HTTPS is terminated at Render's edge and forwarded to the app over plain HTTP) — so the link never worked. Replaced with a short, human-typed 6-character join code that never requires the server to guess a URL at all, and which can now also be generated anytime from an existing workspace's Members page, not just once at creation.
  • Tax Prep Pack coverage is partial — the sales-tax rate table covers 15 states by name (falling back to a flagged national-average estimate otherwise), and the 990/donor-letter logic is federal-level only; state-specific nonprofit registration and filing rules aren't covered at all.
  • The 1099-NEC candidate list is a heuristic — it excludes payroll and internal transfers, but can't know whether a given vendor is incorporated (which would exempt them) — it's explicitly framed as "review these," not a determination.
  • No CAPTCHA or email verification at signup — a reasonable omission for a class project, not for a real public launch.
  • Join codes had no persistent entry point once you're already in a workspace, already fixed: the "Have a join code?" box only lived on the all-workspaces list page, but anyone who belongs to exactly one workspace (most users) gets auto-redirected straight past that page on login — so there was no reachable place in the UI to type a code and join a second workspace. Fixed with a "Join workspace" link always visible in the top nav, pointing at a new /join page that takes just the code and hands off to the existing accept flow.
  • A nonprofit volunteer role not seeing an expected pot, root-caused and fixed: the page's background data loads (pots, members, and others) had no error handling, unlike the one load whose failure was already handled — so a transient failure (a flaky request, a mid-redeploy restart) silently left the page showing "no pots" with nothing to distinguish that from the workspace genuinely having none. Reproduced the volunteer-joins-via-code path end to end afterward and confirmed pots display correctly when the loads succeed; the fix makes a real failure show a visible error instead of quietly rendering empty.
  • The model pricing table is an estimate (Section 5) until the real gpt-6-luna rate is confirmed.

With another month, in priority order: get the Postgres persistence fix above actually deployed and confirmed live on Render (the redeploy needed to pick it up stalled and needs attention on the Render dashboard side); get the pilot feedback above and act on whatever it surfaces; move uploaded receipt files off the web service's own disk onto real object storage (the one piece of storage the Postgres migration above doesn't cover); extend the sales-tax table and 990-adjacent logic state by state; and add CAPTCHA/email verification if this were ever opened up beyond a closed pilot group.
